# A Case for "Incremental" Innovation

One reason to work in health tech is to build cool technology — high-impact projects that inspire you and others, that solve hard problems with advanced techniques. The potential return on investing your life is greater, and it's easier to attract the support you need.

By that logic, building another dialysis product doesn't qualify. Dialysis is expensive, doesn't cure kidney failure, and carries only a 50% five-year median survival rate. The ambitious targets are clear: solutions at least as good as transplants, or interventions that prevent kidney failure altogether.

Several projects are headed in these directions — implantable bio-artificial kidneys, immune-privileged xenotransplants, cryopreservation to expand the organ supply, and therapies targeting CKD progression before it reaches kidney failure. The problem is timelines. The UCSF bio-artificial kidney project has been underway for over 20 years and only recently published animal data. Miromatrix's bio-fabricated kidneys have no reported human studies. Organ cryopreservation remains at the small-animal stage. Xenotransplantation is still in single first-in-human trials. Even the more advanced efforts — like SGLT2 inhibitors being repurposed for kidney care, or ProKidney's cellular therapeutic in Phase II — won't eliminate kidney failure entirely.

The reality is this: kidney failure will continue, and dialysis will remain necessary. Even when the moonshot solutions arrive, not all patients will be eligible for transplants (as is already the case), and dialysis will still serve as a bridge therapy.

So the question becomes: since dialysis isn't going away, what can be done about it *now*?

An estimated 7 million people die each year due to limited access to renal replacement therapy, overwhelmingly in Asia and Africa, where roughly 33% and 16% of patients who need dialysis actually receive it. The scale of the problem is staggering, and the moonshots — however promising — are not arriving in time for these patients.

Others have proposed upstream approaches: better management of hypertension and diabetes (the leading drivers of CKD), and earlier intervention to slow progression to kidney failure. These are worthwhile, but they don't address the patients already at the end stage who need treatment today.

This is where incremental innovation matters. Making existing dialysis technology cheaper, more portable, more accessible, and better suited to resource-limited settings isn't glamorous. It won't land on the cover of a journal or attract the same venture capital enthusiasm as a bioartificial organ. But it directly addresses the gap between patients who need dialysis and patients who can actually get it — a gap that kills millions every year.

The moonshots deserve investment. But the unsexy, incremental work — improving what already exists so it reaches the people who need it most — is the work that saves lives in the meantime.
